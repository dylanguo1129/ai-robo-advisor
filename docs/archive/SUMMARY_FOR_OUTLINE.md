# Step 3 — Optimizer method (summary for the project outline)

*Drop-in replacement for "Step 3 — Optimizer spec" in the Preliminary Project Outline. Full method, evidence and results: `docs/METHOD.md`. Code, tests and outputs: this repository.*

> Archived: this summary describes the v1 optimizer (three risk-aversion settings on the 20-ETF Canadian shortlist). The current engine uses five target-volatility levels; see README.md and docs/PRODUCT_SPEC.md section 7.

**Robo-Kit's optimizer is constrained mean-variance with model-anchored inputs, resampled for stability.** The client's shortlist and one of three risk-aversion settings go in; weights, expected return and volatility, each ETF's share of portfolio risk, and a confidence band per weight come out — in about 30 ms, from sixty lines of NumPy that port directly to JavaScript.

Two choices carry the design. First, expected returns are never historical averages: they are the equilibrium returns implied by what Canadian ETF investors collectively hold (AUM-weighted prior, market risk aversion δ = 2.5), the same reverse-optimization Betterment documents for its portfolios and the "anchor μ to a model" the course insists on. Second, the covariance matrix is shrunk — Ledoit–Wolf linear shrinkage for the prototype, nonlinear (QIS, 2022) once the universe exceeds a quarter of the history length — because a 2026 horse race across eleven covariance estimators still lands on shrinkage for portfolios of this size. The three risk levels are multiples of δ: *mid* means "hold the market portfolio", *high* means "hold less of it and more cash", *low* tilts toward the higher-premium equity ETFs; each can alternatively be calibrated to a target volatility (12 / 9 / 4%).

Robustness is built in rather than claimed: weights are averaged over 500 block-bootstrapped histories (Michaud/Betterment style), client preferences enter as constraints (caps, forced funds, exclusions, group limits) rather than as return forecasts, and a walk-forward test with transaction costs, risk matching and sub-period breakdowns is run under the Arnott–Harvey–Markowitz protocol from Session 2, over seven pre-registered configurations. We report it honestly: against the textbook plug-in optimizer, no configuration produces a statistically significant return difference (t-statistics between −1.7 and +0.5); the plug-in earns more in 2021–2026 and the two are tied over 2018–2026, where Robo-Kit has the smaller drawdowns (−18% vs −22% at mid risk) and half the turnover (21% vs 40% a year). The ranking flips with the estimation window — which is the course's point about single-history back-tests, and why we sell stability and explainability rather than a back-tested Sharpe ratio. Methods evaluated and rejected — hierarchical risk parity, decision-focused learning, neural covariance cleaning, distributionally robust optimization — are documented with sources; they need data we do not have and cannot be explained to the panel.
